# Choosing an Investigation

This document guides you through **how to select an investigative topic** and decide whether it’s worth pursuing.  
Good investigations are not chosen at random — they emerge from patterns, evidence gaps, structural problems, and *questions that can be proven or disproven through research*. :contentReference[oaicite:2]{index=2}

---

## 1. What Makes an Investigative Topic

An investigative topic typically:
- focuses on **systemic issues**, not isolated incidents  
- has an angle that can *be tested with evidence* (documents, data, interviews)  
- addresses **public accountability**, not just personal grievance  
- has **clear stakes** for audiences or affected communities :contentReference[oaicite:3]{index=3}

**Keep this in mind:**  
*A good topic is specific enough to investigate, but broad enough to matter.*

---

## 2. Where Good Ideas Come From

Investigative topics often begin in one of these places:

### A. Patterns that don’t add up  
Repeated outcomes where the *why* isn’t explained — for example, disproportionate spending, repeat audit findings, or regulatory loopholes.

### B. Gaps between policy and practice  
When formal rules or laws exist but **results diverge from promises**.

### C. Community signals  
Persistent concerns raised by affected people — *but verify with records and data,* not just anecdotes.

### D. Documents and public records  
Sometimes the *documents already show the discrepancy* — budgets, court dockets, contracts, filings.

### E. Existing coverage + new angle  
There may be reporting already — but a deeper, evidence-oriented angle is the entry point.

---

## 3. Early Screening Questions

Before committing to a topic, answer these questions:

1. **Can this be supported or tested with evidence?**  
   Are there documents, public records, data, or sources you can reasonably access?

2. **Is there a responsible actor?**  
   Who makes decisions? Who can be held accountable?

3. **Can you articulate the impact?**  
   Who is affected, and why should it matter to readers?

4. **Is the scope manageable?**  
   Too broad → impossible; too narrow → may not be publishable.

5. **Is there urgency or a news hook?**  
   (Not always required, but helpful for publication timing)

If you can *tentatively* answer these with clarity, your topic may be viable.

---

## 4. Framing the Investigative Question

Turn a general idea into a **testable investigative question**:

**Weak:**  
*“Is there corruption in local spending?”*

**Stronger:**  
*“Why did Department X award 7 of 9 contracts to Company Y despite repeated audit flags from 2019-2024?”*

Good investigative questions specify:
- **who**
- **what**
- **when**
- **where**
- and the *central relationship to be tested*.

This framing is central to planning evidence collection.

---

## 5. Basic Stress Tests

Before deep reporting, do quick checks:

### Document Scan  
Look for:
- budgets and line items
- contracts and procurement records
- regulatory filings
- court dockets

What you *don’t* find is often as informative as what you do.

### Timeline  
Build a quick timeline — this helps reveal patterns or anomalies.

### “Disproof” Thinking  
Ask:  
*What evidence would show this idea is false?*  
If you can’t imagine a possible disproof, either it’s too vague or you haven’t thought it through — either way, revise.

---

## 6. Finding Sources Early

Good topic choice involves early exploration of sources:
- officials in relevant institutions
- experts on the topic
- people affected by the issue
- insiders or whistleblowers

Even before formal reporting, conversations help refine the angle.

---

## 7. How To Validate a Topic Rapidly

Rapid validation involves:
- gathering **existing documents**
- sketching a **timeline**
- doing quick **background research**
- assessing whether a critical mass of evidence *might* be obtainable

If early evidence exists that supports or refines the question, proceed.

If early evidence consistently contradicts your premise, reconsider or pivot.

---

## 8. What Editors and Practitioners Look For

Good investigative topics share these traits:
- a **clear question**
- a plausible **evidence base**
- potential for **impact**
- a defensible ethical position

Early planning and focus elevate a topic from “interesting” to *investigative*.

---

## 9. Final Checklist

Before you commit to reporting:

☑ Have you defined your investigative question?  
☑ Can you list at least 3 types of evidence you will seek?  
☑ Do you have initial leads on sources?  
☑ Have you sketched a timeline?  
☑ Can you describe the expected contribution of the investigation?

Passing these checks doesn’t guarantee publication — but it *does* make your topic realistically investigable.
